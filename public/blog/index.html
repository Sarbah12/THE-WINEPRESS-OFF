How Fear and Avoidance Lead to Slothfulness

By Afua

July 2026 · Personal Reflection

"The sluggard says, 'There is a lion outside! I shall be killed in the streets!'"

— Proverbs 22:13 (ESV)

When we hear the word sloth, we often think of laziness. We picture someone who lacks ambition, avoids responsibility, or simply doesn't want to work. But Scripture paints a much deeper picture.

Sloth isn't merely inactivity. It is the neglect of what God has entrusted to us. It is a heart that has slowly drifted from purposeful obedience.

This is why someone can be physically busy and still be spiritually slothful. You can fill your calendar with meetings, serve in every ministry, answer every call, show up for everyone around you, and still neglect the very thing God has asked of you.

Sometimes sloth isn't doing nothing. Sometimes it begins with doing everything except what God actually called you to do.

The Hidden Roots of Sloth

Sloth rarely appears overnight. Like many spiritual conditions, it grows quietly beneath the surface. Sometimes it begins with fear.

Fear rarely announces itself as fear. Instead, it disguises itself as caution, perfectionism, overthinking, or the desire to be "more prepared." "What if I fail?" "What if I'm rejected?" "What if I make the wrong decision?" Instead of moving in faith, we begin to avoid.

Avoidance is fear's closest companion. We tell ourselves we'll start tomorrow. We'll pray later. We'll write the book when we have more experience. We'll obey once we're certain. We don't reject God's invitation — we simply keep postponing it.

The more we avoid, the heavier obedience feels. The heavier obedience feels, the easier it becomes to avoid. Over time, avoidance can harden into sloth.

When Sloth Looks Like Busyness

One of the enemy's greatest strategies is not always to make us stop. Sometimes it is to keep us distracted. Busy enough to avoid stillness. Productive enough to avoid purpose. Occupied enough to never ask whether our lives are aligned with God's will.

Real joy isn't found in being busy. It is found in living the purpose God created you for. There is a weariness that comes from overworking. But there is another kind of weariness that comes from living outside of God's assignment. No amount of activity can satisfy a soul that is avoiding its purpose.

The Things That Feed Sloth

Sometimes sloth grows because we've lost sight of meaning. When we no longer understand why we're here or what God is inviting us into, life begins to feel directionless. Without vision, we drift. Without purpose, we settle.

Other times, sloth hides behind blame shifting and a victim mentality. We begin to believe our circumstances, our upbringing, or the actions of others have permanently disqualified us from living faithfully. While pain is real and injustice leaves scars, remaining in a posture of helplessness keeps us from responding to God's invitation today.

Healing acknowledges what happened. Victimhood convinces us that nothing else can happen. God calls us to healing because healing restores our ability to respond to Him.

Sloth can also grow where there is little emotional awareness. Sometimes what we call laziness is actually unresolved grief. Sometimes it is burnout. Sometimes it is fear. Sometimes it is disappointment with God. If we never slow down long enough to examine our hearts, we may spend years fighting the wrong battle.

How Slothfulness Slowly Sets In

Sloth often begins long before we notice it. Poor boundaries create constant fatigue. Unchecked fatigue makes avoidance feel like relief. Persistent avoidance eventually begins to resemble slothfulness. What started as exhaustion slowly becomes spiritual passivity.

People-pleasing can lead us down the same road. Scripture repeatedly reminds us not to seek the approval of people above the approval of God. When our lives become consumed with managing people's expectations, we begin dragging our feet on the very things God is calling us to in this season. We say yes to everyone else until we no longer have the strength to say yes to God.

The tragedy isn't simply that we're tired. It's that we've become unavailable for the assignment He has given us.

A Lesson from the Parable of the Talents

Perhaps one of the clearest pictures of fear producing sloth is found in Jesus' Parable of the Talents (Matthew 25:14–30). When the master asked the servant why he buried the talent entrusted to him, the servant did not say he lacked ability. He simply said,

"I was afraid."

— Matthew 25:25

Fear convinced him that hiding was safer than stewarding. His problem wasn't a lack of gifting. It was a lack of trust. How often do we bury what God has entrusted to us because fear whispers that it's safer not to try?

The Kingdom of God has never advanced through people who felt completely ready. It has always moved through people who trusted God enough to take the next step.

Walking Out of Sloth

The answer to sloth isn't simply trying harder. It is returning to God. It begins with prayer — not because prayer magically removes fear, but because prayer reminds us who is truly in control. As we pray and immerse ourselves in God's Word, we learn to keep in step with the Holy Spirit rather than our emotions or fears.

It requires intentionality. Purpose doesn't happen by accident. We must choose, day after day, to orient our lives toward the things God has called us to, even when they feel costly.

And it requires introspection. Ask the Holy Spirit to search your heart. Is it truly laziness? Or is it fear? Is it burnout? Is it grief? Is it people-pleasing? Is it a lack of boundaries? Healing begins where honesty does.

Questions to ask in prayer

  • Is it truly laziness — or is it fear, burnout, grief, or people-pleasing?
  • What has been keeping me from faithfully stewarding what God has placed in my hands?
  • Am I busy with everything except what God actually called me to do?
  • Where have avoidance and postponement hardened into spiritual passivity?

Fear buries. Avoidance delays. Sloth settles. But the Spirit leads us into faithful obedience.

May we not merely be busy people. May we be faithful stewards of the life, the gifts, and the purpose God has entrusted to us.

What God Is Teaching Me About Trying Times

By Afua

2025 · Personal Reflection

Among the nine parts of the fruit of the Spirit, my least favourite is long-suffering. To suffer long means "having or showing patience despite troubles, especially those caused by other people." Sometimes, I feel it's unfair to endure this, particularly when it results from others' actions.

I'm realising that for a Christian, patience is an important aspect of the journey. It is not a passive thing — it is active, costly, and often deeply uncomfortable. And yet, it is the very thing God keeps bringing me back to.

"If it is possible, as far as it depends on you, live at peace with everyone."

— Romans 12:18

In Romans 12:18, we are encouraged to live peacefully with everyone — if possible, as much as it depends on us. This implies that as Christians, we should actively seek peace and pursue it, as stated in Psalm 34:14. Peace is not something that just happens. We pursue it. We choose it. Even when it is not chosen in return.

The Lord is teaching me how to respond well in tough seasons. The Scriptures remind us that our sufferings produce perseverance; perseverance, character; and character, hope. (Romans 5:3–4) We must know that our suffering is not in vain. God has a purpose for it — even when we cannot trace it from where we are standing.

"So in seasons when I feel like my faith is being tested and I'm going through things that do not feel pleasant, I encourage myself in the Lord."

When provoked — and we will be provoked — I am learning that I must not respond in a rash manner. The tendency is to react immediately, to say the thing, to send the message, to prove the point. But the Word is clear:

"Sensible people control their temper; they earn respect by overlooking wrongs."

— Proverbs 19:11

I am also learning to give thanks in all circumstances — not just the pleasant ones. When going through tough times, gratitude is not denial. It is not pretending things are fine when they are not. It is a deliberate act of trust that says: God is still God, and He is still good, even in this.

Gratitude keeps me calm. It keeps my head level when everything around me is pressing. Instead of complaining and grumbling throughout the season — which only makes it heavier — I am choosing to look for what God is doing, even when I cannot see the full picture.

What I am learning in this season

  • Patience is not passive — it is an active, daily, costly choice rooted in trust.
  • When provoked, respond slowly. Sensible people control their temper and earn respect by overlooking wrongs.
  • Give thanks in all circumstances. Gratitude is a discipline, not a feeling — it steadies you in the storm.
  • Our suffering is not in vain. God has a purpose for every pressing season, even the ones that feel pointless.

This is not a blog post that ends with a perfectly tied bow. I am still in the middle of learning this. Some days I get it right. Some days I send the message I should not have sent, or I let the frustration win. But God is faithful to keep teaching — and I am committed to keep learning.

If you are in a trying season right now — one where patience feels impossible and peace feels far away — I hope this reflection is a reminder that you are not alone in the process. He is still working. Even here. Even now.
